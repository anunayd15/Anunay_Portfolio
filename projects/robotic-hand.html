Morphing Robotic Hand

WHAT WAS THE PROBLEM?

Existing robotic end-effectors were too specialized for flexible manipulation research

The MINIMAX Lab's KUKA robotic arm needed an end-effector capable of interacting with objects of different sizes, shapes, and task requirements without requiring frequent hardware changes.

Commercial robotic hands often trade off between grip span, dexterity, adaptability, mechanical complexity, and cost. Many systems are optimized around specific tasks, which limits their flexibility in research environments where different grasping and manipulation behaviors may need to be explored using the same platform.

The goal was therefore to create a single modular robotic hand capable of changing its geometry and functionality while remaining practical to prototype and modify in-house.

PROJECT OBJECTIVES

Adaptable Grasping

Grasp objects across a range of sizes, from smaller objects such as a tennis ball to larger objects such as a basketball.

Reconfigurable Geometry

Rotate and reposition the fingers around the palm to support different grasp configurations.

Joint Stiffness

Mechanically constrain individual finger joints when greater rigidity is required.

Tool Interaction

Integrate a powered tool directly into the hand to expand functionality beyond object grasping.

EARLY PROTOTYPES

Early concepts revealed limitations that shaped the final design

WHAT DIDN'T WORK?

Direct actuation added too much complexity

Early design concepts explored placing actuators closer to individual finger joints. While this approach could improve direct control, it increased finger size, mass, and mechanical complexity. The addition of actuators at each joint would have definitely made each finger too bulky. This pushed the design toward centralized tendon-driven actuation.

Tendon actuation alone did not provide enough stiffness

Tendon-driven fingers improved adaptability but introduced compliance. Simply holding motor position was not sufficient to keep the finger rigid under external loading, which motivated the addition of mechanical joint locking using a solenoid.

Manufacturing tests required continuous design iterations

Early 3D-prints exposed issues that were difficult to fully predict in CAD, including weak features, interference between mating components, and insufficient space for hardware and tendon routing. As the system was assembled, designs were revised to improve clearances, accommodate motors and tendon paths, and strengthen load-bearing features. Print settings such as wall count, infill, layer height, and part orientation were also adjusted to improve component strength and reliability.

SYSTEM DESIGN

Tendon-Driven Actuation

Centralized motors pull tendon lines routed through the slots inside the finger to create flexion across multiple joints. This keeps the fingers lighter and slimmer than a design with motors positioned directly at every joint.

Finger Rotation

A worm-gear mechanism rotates the fingers around the palm so the hand can transition between different grasp configurations. The gearing also helps maintain finger orientation without requiring continuous actuator power.

Radial Extension

A linear actuation mechanism changes the radial position of the finger, expanding the hand's grip span so it can accommodate objects of significantly different sizes.

Joint Locking

A custom solenoid drives a toothed clutch into engagement at each joint. This allows the finger to remain compliant during movement and then become mechanically constrained when greater stiffness is needed.

Fingertip Screwdriver

A small N20 motor and miniature chuck integrate a powered screwdriver directly into the fingertip. Embedding the tool avoids the need for the hand to grasp and manipulate an external screwdriver.

Electronics & Control

An Arduino Mega coordinates the hand's motors through multiple motor drivers, while encoder feedback provides position information from the actuators. The electronics architecture allows the mechanical subsystems to be controlled together as a single integrated system.

OUTCOME

A functional proof of concept for reconfigurable robotic manipulation

The final prototype demonstrated grasping across different object sizes, multi-degree-of-freedom reconfiguration, mechanical joint locking, and integrated tool operation.

The completed system showed that a modular robotic hand could combine adaptable grasping and multiple mechanical functions using relatively simple actuators and manufactured components.

The final prototype also provided the MINIMAX Lab with a proof-of-concept robotic hand that can serve as a foundation for continued development to conduct future experimentations on their KUKA robotic arm.
